About this project

Can a theory of the mind actually move a hand?

This is an experimental web page. On it, two virtual hands sit over a keyboard. Their fingers are controlled by computational models from cognitive science that try to explain how people plan and produce skilled sequences of actions, like typing a sentence.

The puzzle

Watch a skilled typist and their fingers seem to fly over the keys on their own. A practised pianist or guitarist does the same thing. Behind that ease is a remarkable piece of mental machinery: the brain has to decide which action comes next, in what order, and when, and then turn those decisions into precisely timed movements of muscles, tendons, and finger joints.

Cognitive scientists have built many computational models of the first part, the planning and ordering of actions. Some of these models are decades old, and a few describe action all the way down to the level of hands and fingers. But the link between the model and the body is usually left vague. A model might say "press J now" and stop there. It never has to work out how a real index finger, attached to a real hand, would actually get to the J key in time, or what happens when the ring finger refuses to move without dragging the little finger along with it.

The question behind this page: if you take the models of action sequencing that researchers have been using and hook them up to a realistic pair of hands, can they actually do the job? Could they plausibly control human hands?

Why build it now?

Building a detailed 3D simulation of human hands used to be a large engineering project in its own right. While working on this research, it became clear that AI coding tools (in this case, Anthropic's Claude) made it possible to build one quickly: a skeleton of the hand and arm, a keyboard with real geometry, and a way to plug different cognitive models into the same body and compare them.

That turns an old theoretical question into something you can test. A model that types beautifully in theory might stumble once it has to move fingers that have limits, that are coupled to each other, and that take time to travel. Or it might show human-like patterns of speed and error that it was never explicitly designed to produce. Either result tells us something about the theory.

What you're looking at

  • The mindA cognitive model decides which key comes next and starts preparing the keys after it.
  • The motor systemThat decision is turned into a planned movement of a particular finger, with a little noise, just as real movements have.
  • The bodySimulated hands, built bone by bone, with realistic joint limits and fingers that can't move fully independently.
  • The keyboardA key only registers if a fingertip actually pushes it down far enough. Slips onto neighbouring keys are real.

Four models are included so far: Rumelhart and Norman's classic 1982 typing simulation, a "competitive queuing" model, a hierarchical model in which an outer loop handles words and an inner loop handles keystrokes, and an instance-memory model (Minerva 2) that learns to type from stored experiences. More learning models will be added.

How to explore it

  • Press Run and watch the hands type the sentence.
  • Pick a different cognitive model from the menu and run it again on the same hands.
  • Drag on the 3D view to look around, and scroll to zoom in on the fingers.
  • Keys glow blue while the model is getting ready to press them and orange when they are pressed.
  • The charts on the right compare the simulated typist's timing with human typists, for example how much faster people are when consecutive letters use alternate hands.
  • Turn up the motor noise or the speed and see what kinds of typing errors appear.

Where this is going

This is an early, working prototype, and it's meant to be explored, not taken as a finished result. The hands are currently a detailed skeleton without muscles. Future versions aim to add realistic physics, muscles and tendons, more models of learning, comparisons against large sets of human typing data, and eventually other skills such as playing the piano. The project plan and the source code are open.

Who made this

MC

Matt Crump is a cognitive psychologist and Professor of Psychology at Brooklyn College of the City University of New York. Part of his research program asks how people learn skills such as action sequencing: learning to type on a computer keyboard, or to play the piano or guitar. His lab has studied skilled typing for many years, along with computational models of learning and memory.
